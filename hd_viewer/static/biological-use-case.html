Which cells surround the tumor?

A colorectal tumor develops within a tissue containing immune cells, fibroblasts, blood vessels, and other populations. Their location matters: a population abundant across the section may be scarce at the tumor boundary, while a smaller population may concentrate there. Understanding this microenvironment therefore requires asking both which cells are present and where they are relative to the tumor.

In High-definition spatial transcriptomic profiling of immune cell populations in colorectal cancer, Oliveira et al. (Nature Genetics 57, 1512–1523, 2025) investigate human colorectal cancer using Visium HD. They combine spatial gene expression with a single-cell reference to infer cell identities while retaining their tissue locations. This connects the molecular diversity of the tumor microenvironment to its spatial organization, which cannot be recovered from cell-type abundance alone.

What the authors did

For Figure 4a–b, the authors analyze 8 × 8 µm capture bins, identify tumor regions from RCTD deconvolution labels, and select a 50 µm periphery around them, excluding the tumor bins themselves. They compare cell-type proportions in this neighborhood with those in the remaining tissue and inspect C1QC and COL1A1 expression as supporting evidence for macrophage and fibroblast localization. They report cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) as the most prominent periphery cell type and macrophages as its most abundant immune population. These findings motivate the subsequent macrophage analyses in the paper.

What we will try to replicate

We will follow the reasoning behind Figure 4a–b for patient P1CRC only, moving from a whole-section view to a defined tumor neighborhood and then to a quantitative comparison:

  1. Reconstruct the spatial view in panel a: identify the P1CRC tumor population, define its 50 µm periphery, and examine C1QC and COL1A1 in the same tissue region.
  2. Examine the composition comparison in panel b: assess the representation of CAFs, macrophages, and other annotated populations near the tumor relative to the remaining tissue, keeping the counting rules explicit.

The aim is to understand how the spatial evidence supports the reported pattern and assess whether it is recovered in this sample. The other patients and the macrophage subpopulation analyses in panels c–d are outside this walkthrough.

Why explore interactively?

A published figure presents selected views and summaries. To assess the result, we also need to inspect the tissue behind those summaries: whether inferred tumor regions follow the histology, whether marker expression occurs along the boundary or in isolated patches, and which bins enter the comparison. These checks matter because a capture bin can contain transcripts from several cells, and choices about labels, neighborhood membership, and denominators affect the biological interpretation.

An interactive viewer makes those checks practical. Zooming connects a section-wide pattern to individual boundary areas; switching between cell-type labels and gene expression lets us examine complementary evidence at the same location; and a visible ROI makes the selected neighborhood inspectable. Regional summaries then connect what we see on the map to measured values. This helps researchers examine the assumptions behind a result and identify discrepancies that need further analysis.

Explore, then document. Follow the published 50 µm definition first. If you later vary the radius or tumor labels, treat that as a sensitivity analysis and record each choice. Interactive inspection supports reproducibility when the data, parameters, region definitions, and readouts remain explicit.
The steps below provide an exploratory reproduction in the viewer. Matching the published numbers also requires the original bin classifications, boundary filtering, and singlet-only counting described below. The walkthrough does not claim that a new viewer run has reproduced those numbers.

Source: paper, Figure 4a–b and Methods: “Characterizing the tumor periphery”.

Data and prerequisites

Download the public data: visit the 10x Genomics human colorectal cancer dataset page and select the patient P1 colorectal cancer (P1CRC) sample. For this walkthrough, obtain the Space Ranger binned outputs and tissue image; use the 8 µm expression matrix and matching spatial positions. The study's Chromium reference data are also available through this collection, and the authors provide cell-type annotations and deconvolution metadata in their repository.

We recommend using the preloaded example. On the viewer's loading screen, select the built-in Human CRC example to load the P1CRC data directly, with no download or upload needed. Running spacexr (RCTD) from scratch on this many bins is a long-running job: it can take approximately 7–8 hours, depending on the server hardware and settings. A run continues only while your browser session stays open, so keep the tab open until it finishes. Where the server provides cached results for the example, a run with the same input data, reference, and parameters reuses them instead of repeating the computation; changing any of these starts a new run. Alternatively, download the public files and load them through the own-data upload.

1 · Load P1CRC

  1. On the loading screen, choose the Human CRC example dataset. If using your own downloaded copy instead, upload the P1CRC files listed above and set the bin resolution to 8 µm.
  2. Use the Bins layer. Pseudospots and cell segmentation are unnecessary for Figure 4a–b, which analyzes 8 µm capture bins.
  3. Load the sample and confirm that the bins align with the H&E tissue. For close inspection, set bin resolution to the native level (no aggregation).

The supplied positions file contains coordinates, not the authors' RCTD labels or periphery assignments. Those annotations must be computed or prepared separately.

2 · Identify the P1CRC tumor population

  1. In the bins layer's Deconvolution tab, change Reference cell-type column (.obs) from the example's default Level1 to Level2.
  2. Choose spacexr (RCTD), set RCTD mode to doublet, and Minimum target UMIs to 100, as in the paper. Keep raw integer counts as input. Record the remaining settings and reference preparation; they also affect the result.
  3. Click Run deconvolution and retain that saved run throughout the walkthrough.
  4. In Groups, choose the run's dominant-type metadata column, e.g. deconv_1:most_abundant for the first run. Add the condition == Tumor II and create a group named P1 tumor.

The authors' Figure_4.R uses Tumor II for P1CRC. Use this subtype rather than pooling all tumor labels.

The viewer's dominant-type column chooses the largest fraction among assigned types, including confident doublets. It is not the original RCTD singlet classification. Do not infer singlet status from a dominant label or a high fraction.

Help with deconvolution →

3 · Define the 50 µm tumor periphery

  1. Open ROIs on the bins layer and choose Seed + radius.
  2. Set Seed source to All bins in a group, and choose P1 tumor.
  3. Confirm the radius unit is µm and enter 50. If the control reports pixels, resolve image calibration before continuing.
  4. Set ROI region to ROI minus group. This removes the source tumor bins from the surrounding neighborhood.
  5. Name the region P1 periphery 50um, inspect the preview, click Save ROI, then use its Create group button.

Checkpoint: the region follows tumor-associated areas across the section, including disconnected areas, rather than a single circle around one click.

The paper's method suppresses isolated seeds with fewer than 25 same-label bins in the local neighborhood. The viewer does not apply this filter. Its ROI membership uses bin-shape intersection, whereas the authors' helper uses distances between bin centers. Edge bins can therefore differ even with the same radius.

4 · Inspect C1QC and COL1A1 — Figure 4a

  1. Show the tumor and periphery groups. Use red for tumor and blue for periphery to echo the paper's map, and inspect both the section overview and a boundary close-up.
  2. In Color, display C1QC to inspect the macrophage-associated signal. Then display COL1A1 to inspect fibroblast-associated expression.
  3. Keep the same viewport for both genes. Compare their localization with the saved periphery outline and H&E morphology.

For the published close-up, the figure script specifies a roughly 500 µm square centered on barcode s_008um_00556_00532-1. To go there, open ROIs, choose Seed + radius, set Seed source to Find barcode / bin index, and type or paste the barcode. The viewer previews a radius region around that bin and brings it into view; a radius of 250 µm approximates the published close-up. Click Cancel to discard the draft once the view is positioned, or save it if you want to return to the same area.

The paper shows normalized log expression. The viewer's ordinary gene display reads the loaded count matrix, so use it to compare spatial patterns; its color intensity is not the paper's normalized expression scale. Neither marker alone establishes a cell's identity.

5 · Compare periphery and remaining tissue — Figure 4b

  1. Keep only the two custom groups created above: P1 tumor and P1 periphery 50um. Delete unrelated custom groups before this comparison.
  2. Open Metadata comparison. Select the periphery group and enable The rest (outside all defined custom groups and the compared set). With these two groups defined, the remainder excludes both tumor and periphery.
  3. Choose the same saved deconvolution run's fractions in Metadata set. Include at least CAF and Macrophage, and choose Summary = Mean.
  4. Click Compare metadata. Inspect the bars below the map and hover for the values and numbers of finite observations.

Readout: these are mean inferred fractions, not percentages of singlet bins. Confident doublets contribute to these means; unassigned values are omitted. Showing fewer cell types does not renormalize their fractions.

Hiding an unrelated custom group does not restore its members to The rest; every defined group affects that remainder. An empty remainder cannot be compared.

To match the published bin proportions

Panel b counts singlet bins by cell-type label. For each region R and cell type C:

Proportion (%) = 100 × singlet bins labeled C in R / all singlet bins in R

The figure script separates tumor, periphery, and remaining tissue before counting. Tumor is excluded from the gray tissue comparison. The denominator includes all singlet types in each compared region, even when only selected types are plotted.

For numerical reproduction, use the authors' P1CRC deconvolution results and boundary helper, or retain the equivalent original RCTD classification output. Match labels by barcode, use the same in-tissue and missing-data filters, and apply the neighbor filter and center-distance rule. The viewer's current result columns do not expose singlet status.

To inspect those counts in this viewer, prepare a separate copy of the positions Parquet outside the app, preserving its required coordinate and barcode columns. Join the following metadata by barcode, then load that copy through the own-data upload:

  • Numeric flags paper_tumor and paper_periphery (0 or 1), using the published region definitions.
  • One numeric indicator per cell type: 1 for singlets of that type, 0 for other singlets, and NaN for every non-singlet or ineligible bin. Use the same eligible singlets across all indicator columns.

Create just two groups with paper_tumor == 1 and paper_periphery == 1. Compare the latter to The rest using Metadata set = Numeric metadata, select the indicator columns, and choose Mean. Multiply these means by 100 to obtain percentages. This avoids treating mixture weights as singlet counts.

Annotation preparation and the original boundary calculation are external steps; the standard Human CRC example does not supply these extra columns.

ReproducibilityRecord the reference/QC choices, Level2 labels, RCTD parameters, tumor definition, radius, membership rule, eligible bin counts, and comparison readout.

If results differ, first check Level2 versus Level1, Tumor II versus all tumor subtypes, the 100-UMI cutoff, reference QC, and whether you are comparing fractions or singlet counts. Then check distance calibration, isolated-seed filtering, and the groups defining The rest. Numerical values from a new run must be measured; no patient-specific percentages are assumed here.

Interpretation and references

This analysis describes which annotated capture bins occupy the tumor neighborhood in one P1CRC tissue section. It supports investigation of macrophage and fibroblast localization; spatial proximity alone does not demonstrate a functional interaction. Bin proportions also differ from physical cell counts, and the 8 µm bins can mix signals from small immune cells and adjacent populations.

Primary source: Oliveira et al., High-definition spatial transcriptomic profiling of immune cell populations in colorectal cancer, Nature Genetics 57, 1512–1523 (2025). See Figure 4a–b and Methods: “Spot deconvolution” and “Characterizing the tumor periphery” (doi:10.1038/s41588-025-02193-3).
